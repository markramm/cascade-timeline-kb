The New York Times Company and its reporter Julian E. Barnes sued the Department of Defense and Secretary Pete Hegseth in the District of Columbia on the date of this entry, challenging the Pentagon's press-credential policy of October 6, 2025. The Times's complaint is the source for what follows, and where it quotes the policy or the Department, the quotation is the complaint's.

**What the policy required.** The policy conditioned a press credential on signing an acknowledgment that the reporter had read and understood the Department's in-brief for media members. The acknowledgment says the signature represents understanding "even if I do not necessarily agree with such policies and procedures." The policy did not require approval before publication on its face. The Times alleges that it let officials suspend or revoke a credential on a determination that a journalist was a "security or safety risk," and that the in-brief treats a journalist who solicits non-public information, or encourages Department personnel to violate disclosure rules, as one. Pentagon spokesman Sean Parnell wrote in a September 24 letter, as the complaint quotes it, that the disclosure restriction "does not impose restrictions on journalistic activities, such as investigating, reporting, or publishing stories." Earlier entries and this entry's earlier title called it a publication-approval requirement; the Times's own complaint asks, through the Reporters Committee's letter, whether the acknowledgment meant that, and records the Department's denial.

**Who it applied to.** Every reporter holding a Pentagon credential, not the Times alone. Credential holders were given until October 14 to sign. The complaint says that journalists at the Times and "virtually every other major news organization" declined, and that of the 56 news outlets in the Pentagon Press Association only one agreed to sign.

**What followed.** The Times's complaint in a second suit says Judge Paul L. Friedman concluded in the first suit that the policy "violated the First and Fifth Amendments," vacated its unconstitutional provisions and enjoined their enforcement against the Times and Barnes. It adds that the Department then appealed, and the D.C. Circuit granted a "limited stay pending appeal" that let a later escort requirement take effect. The ruling in the first suit has its own entry.

This entry's earlier title and text said the policy required Pentagon approval before publication, said the Times was singled out, and drew conclusions about the Department's purpose from the Times's press coverage.
