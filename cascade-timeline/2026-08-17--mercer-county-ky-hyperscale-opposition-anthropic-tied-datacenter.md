More than 200 residents packed a Mercer County planning and zoning hearing in Harrodsburg, Kentucky in late July 2026 to oppose a proposed hyperscale data center, in a fight Louisville Public Media reported on August 17 as bipartisan — Republicans and Democrats in the same room against the same project. The grassroots group organizing the opposition, We Are Mercer County, claims more than 1,000 members in a county of roughly 22,000. LPM's reporting was picked up across the regional public-radio network, including West Virginia Public Broadcasting, which is itself a signal about how far outside Kentucky the story was read as relevant.

The following day, August 18, LPM reported separately that a developer of a Kentucky data center with disclosed ties to Anthropic said the project meets the state's new limits on environmental and energy impact. That is the reported claim and the reported tie; LPM did not independently verify the compliance assertion, and no other outlet has corroborated it. It is worth logging precisely because the corpus's data-center entries have overwhelmingly named Meta, Google, Microsoft, Amazon, and OpenAI as the demand side — Effingham County residents confronting OpenAI's Project Camellia, Archer County denying Google's Project Raptor ([[2026-06-22--archer-county-tx-denies-project-raptor-google-abatement-2800-acres]]), Fulton County unwinding Microsoft abatements ([[2026-08-09--fulton-county-ga-votes-5-0-oppose-data-center-abatements-microsoft-unwinds]]). An Anthropic-tied buildout in Kentucky is the first appearance of that name in the siting record.

Mercer County is the demand-side counterpart to the supply-side fight already in canon: Louisville Metro Council's six-month moratorium, passed 24-1 on August 13 ([[2026-08-13--fort-worth-louisville-consecutive-datacenter-moratorium-votes]]), froze new approvals in the state's largest city while a rural county 60 miles southeast was fighting a single project through zoning. Kentucky has not enacted large-load tariff protections, which leaves the ratepayer question — who pays for the interconnection and generation a hyperscale campus requires — unresolved at the state level and pushed down onto county planning commissions with no authority to answer it. The bipartisan composition of the Mercer opposition is what that unresolved question looks like politically: the cost falls on everyone regardless of party.
