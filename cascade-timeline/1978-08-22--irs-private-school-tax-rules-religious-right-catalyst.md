On August 22, 1978, the Internal Revenue Service published a proposed revenue procedure on private tax-exempt schools (43 F.R. 37296 through 37298). The Supreme Court wrote that "the IRS reviewed its challenged policies and proposed new Revenue Procedures to tighten requirements for eligibility for tax-exempt status for private schools". A contemporaneous news report put it this way: private schools in areas that were integrating "may be presumed to be discriminatory if their minority enrollment is not at least 20 percent of the local school age minority population". A second proposed revenue procedure on private tax-exempt schools is dated February 13, 1979 (44 F.R. 9451 through 9455).

**Response.** An Associated Press dispatch, relayed in a periodical, put the protest at "over 100,000 letters of protest".

**Congress.** In P.L. 96-74, approved September 29, 1979, SEC. 103 barred funds for any rule or standard "unless in effect prior to August 22, 1978", and another section barred funds to carry out either proposed revenue procedure on private tax-exempt schools. The bar ran for one fiscal year. The Supreme Court held that "These amendments therefore do not indicate Congressional rejection of Revenue Ruling 71-447".

**Who attributes the Religious Right's start to this fight.** Historian Randall Balmer argues that the tax-exemption fight, not abortion, started the Religious Right; he dates it to "the 1975 action by the IRS against Bob Jones University", not to the 1978 procedure. He reports that at a conference in November 1990 Paul Weyrich said "what got us going as a political movement was the attempt on the part of the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) to rescind the tax-exempt status of Bob Jones University because of its racially discriminatory policies"; Balmer gives that sentence without quotation marks. Balmer writes that the policy was "mandated by Nixon". Jonathan Whitehead, in a guest post for The Gospel Coalition, argues that the "triggering event" was "anti-discrimination Evangelicals outraged by an IRS plan" to presume church schools segregationist, and writes that "The religious right surfaced in the 1978 midterm elections".

- The text of the 1978 procedure and its test were not read here. The enrollment threshold is now tied to one news item.
- The sentence that the publication set off the mass mobilization that created the Religious Right is cut. It is Balmer's thesis and now appears as his.
- Balmer gives Weyrich's remark about what got the movement going without quotation marks; it now appears that way. The line about trying other issues and failing was dated to a 1990 conference, following Politico; Balmer's 2006 book puts it in an interview in the early 1990s, in different words. Cut as unsourced: Weyrich on Christians isolating themselves in their own institutions, and Weyrich on organizing a movement around defending racial segregation (the nearest sentence in Politico is Balmer's own prose).
- Cut for lack of a source: hearings on segregated Christian academies beginning with this action; Weyrich launching the Moral Majority with Falwell; the Moral Majority fundraising and voter-registration figures (the sources to carry them are its own filings or a named history); Viguerie, Phillips and Dolan as part of that account; and the paragraphs tying White Citizens' Councils, segregation academies and massive resistance to later conservative messaging, which are interpretation without a source.

- The letter count was a range taken from two Wikipedia pages; the higher figure was not located again, and no IRS count turned up.
