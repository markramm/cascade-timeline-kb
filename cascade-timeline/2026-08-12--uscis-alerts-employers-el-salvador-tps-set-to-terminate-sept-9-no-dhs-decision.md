USCIS published an alert to E-Verify employers on August 12, 2026 stating that "the Temporary Protected Status (TPS) designation of El Salvador and related benefits are set to terminate on Sept. 9, 2026." The notice instructs employers completing Form I-9 to enter September 9, 2026 in the "Expiration Date (if any)" field and to enter that same date when running a case in E-Verify. It also tells Salvadoran TPS holders with pending EAD renewal applications that their expired work permits — those bearing expiration dates of March 9, 2025; June 30, 2024; December 31, 2022; October 4, 2021; January 4, 2021; January 2, 2020; September 9, 2019; or March 9, 2018 — are automatically extended "until Sept. 9, 2026, the end of the El Salvador TPS designation," with notices mailed and posted to myUSCIS accounts.

**What the notice does not say is the story.** It is not a termination announcement. It describes the existing designation's scheduled expiration date and tells employers how to code that date into the verification system; DHS has issued no decision on whether the designation will be extended or allowed to lapse. Under the TPS statute the Secretary must announce an extension-or-termination decision in advance of expiration, and reporting places that deadline for El Salvador at **July 11, 2026** — a date that passed with no announcement posted. Documented NY, reporting on August 21, framed the resulting question directly in its headline: whether USCIS is sending genuine warnings about work-permit expiration or empty threats. It is not resolvable from the face of the document whether this is a mandatory administrative notice preceding a termination already decided, or a pressure signal issued into a vacuum while an expected extension goes unannounced. Roughly 170,000 Salvadorans hold the status, by figures the National Immigration Forum cites; the corpus should not record that their protection "has been terminated," because as of this alert it has not been.

The ambiguity is itself the enforcement instrument, and it operates on employers before it operates on anyone's immigration file. An E-Verify entry of September 9, 2026 is a date in a compliance system that runs on automated flags, and employers reading a federal alert that a status is "set to terminate" have no way to distinguish a scheduled expiration awaiting a decision from a decided lapse. The predictable outcome — preemptive terminations, withdrawn job offers, quiet non-renewals — arrives whether or not DHS ever signs anything, at no administrative cost and with no reviewable action to challenge. That pattern is visible across the TPS docket in this corpus, where terminations that were fought in court took months and generated records: the Supreme Court's stays in the Venezuelan litigation ([[2025-05-19--scotus-stays-noem-v-ntpsa-venezuelan-tps-termination]], [[2025-10-03--scotus-stays-ntpsa-venezuelan-tps-summary-judgment]]), the Ninth Circuit's holding that the Venezuela termination was unlawful ([[2026-01-28--ninth-circuit-affirms-tps-venezuela-unlawful]]), Noem's Yemen termination ([[2026-02-13--noem-terminates-tps-yemen-deportation]]), the Ethiopia termination noticed in the Federal Register in December 2025 and taking effect six days after this alert ([[2026-08-18--dhs-terminates-ethiopia-tps-effective-august-18-2026]]), and the Haiti and Syria cases the Court took ([[2026-03-16--scotus-grants-cert-haiti-syria-tps]]) and then resolved in *Mullin v. Doe* by barring judicial review altogether ([[2026-06-25--scotus-mullin-v-doe-ends-haiti-syria-tps-judicial-review-barred]]). With review foreclosed, silence past a statutory deadline costs the agency nothing and forfeits nothing, while the calendar does the work a signed termination would have had to survive litigation to accomplish.
