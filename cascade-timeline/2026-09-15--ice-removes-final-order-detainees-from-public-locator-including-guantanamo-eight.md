On **September 15, 2026**, ICE stopped disclosing the locations of **thousands of detainees subject to final orders of removal**, removing them from its **Online Detainee Locator System** without announcement. An **Associated Press** investigation published **September 21** established the change, sourced to current and former officials familiar with the practice. Among those no longer findable are **eight Somali men** held at the U.S. military base at **Guantanamo Bay**. Their ACLU attorney, **My Khanh Ngo**, said the change could make it "nearly impossible" for people to challenge their removals in court.

**The locator is the only public mechanism for finding a detained person, and that is what makes its narrowing consequential rather than merely procedural.** An attorney cannot file a habeas petition without knowing the district of confinement; a family cannot retain counsel for someone whose facility they cannot name. Withdrawing the location of people under final orders removes it precisely for the population with the least remaining time — those whose removal can proceed unless a court intervenes. The practical effect is not that review is denied but that the clock runs while counsel searches.

**This is the fourth distinct disclosure withdrawal canon has recorded in four months, and the pattern is the finding.** The Venturella memo eliminated post-release death reporting ([[2026-06-04--venturella-memo-eliminates-post-release-death-reporting-ice-accountability-erasure]]), a policy whose effect canon then documented in a specific case at Delaney Hall ([[2026-08-12--chajon-raxon-third-delaney-hall-death-post-release-non-reporting-policy]]); California's record detention deaths arrived alongside reduced disclosure ([[2026-05-15--california-ice-detention-deaths-record-high-staffing-crisis-reduced-disclosure]]); detention-center reviews were removed from Google ([[2026-07-20--google-censoring-ice-detention-center-reviews]]); and a county detention board closed public comment ([[2026-07-22--ccno-northwest-ohio-board-blocks-public-comment]]). None of these is a change to detention authority. Each removes a channel through which the exercise of that authority could be observed — deaths, conditions, comment, and now location. **An unobservable system is not thereby lawful; it is unauditable, which is a different and more durable condition.**

**Guantanamo is the sharpest instance because it compounds two obscurities.** Offshore military detention already sits outside the ordinary facility-inspection and visitation regime; removing its occupants from the locator means the only public record that a named person is held there is the reporting itself. Eight men are identifiable as a count, not as individuals — and per the source's own framing, their counsel is the party raising the access problem.

**Sourcing note.** Both cited sources are the same AP exclusive as syndicated by two outlets; this is **one tier-1 investigation, not two independent ones**, and should be read that way. The AP attributes the change to "current and former officials familiar with the practice," which is the sourcing as published — no ICE policy document has surfaced. **The event date is September 15, when the locator change took effect, not September 21, the publication date.** The word "thousands" is the AP's characterization; no precise count has been released.

**Open:** the internal ICE directive or guidance authorizing the change, which may predate September 15 by weeks and is the FOIA target; which facilities hold the now-hidden population, and whether Guantanamo is one of a small set of offshore or otherwise unlisted sites; and whether a docket-visible spike in habeas filings or in motions to compel disclosure of location follows — that would be the measurable downstream effect, and the strongest available evidence of the practical harm.

**Named individuals note:** the eight Somali men are not named in the reporting, and no name should be supplied for them. My Khanh Ngo is counsel, an institutional actor, not a harm-subject.
