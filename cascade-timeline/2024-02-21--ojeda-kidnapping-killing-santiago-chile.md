## Opening paragraph

Shortly after 3:05 a.m. on February 21, 2024, four men disguised as Chilean police officers pulled up to an apartment tower in Santiago in a stolen Nissan sedan. Three of them broke into the 14th-floor apartment of Ronald Ojeda, a 32-year-old former Venezuelan army lieutenant who had been granted asylum in Chile, handcuffed him in front of his wife and son, and dragged him out barefoot and in his underwear. His captors tortured him to death at a hideout, according to court documents, and buried his partially dismembered remains in a suitcase beneath a newly poured cement floor. A tip led Chilean police to the buried remains nine days later. The killing is documented fact, independently corroborated across Chilean court records, security video, and multiple tier-1 outlets. **Who ordered it, and specifically whether Venezuelan Interior and Justice Minister Diosdado Cabello personally directed it, is not.** That allegation rests on witness testimony from accused co-conspirators, reported by ProPublica from the Chilean investigative file — not a judicial finding, not a Chilean indictment naming Cabello, and not an ICC finding. This entry states each claim at the evidentiary level the record actually supports.

## What Happened / Key Facts

**The killing (documented).** Ojeda, a dissident who had been jailed and tortured in Venezuela in 2017 and had taken part in a failed 2023 attempt to instigate a military uprising against Nicolás Maduro, was living under asylum in Santiago when he was abducted and killed. Weeks before the kidnapping, the Maduro regime had publicly declared him a traitor. Fingerprints recovered from the abandoned getaway vehicle were traced to a member of Tren de Aragua, the Venezuelan gang. Chilean investigators — per ProPublica's review of court documents, security video, and interviews — quickly concluded the killing was a political operation, not a random crime. Chief prosecutor Héctor Barros: "Ojeda had already escaped from them at least once before. The regime took that personally. He was a high-priority target." (Source: ProPublica, July 10, 2026.)

**The accused organizer — court record vs. allegation.** Chilean prosecutors identify Rafael Enrique Gámez Salas, an accused Tren de Aragua "boss" of a Santiago offshoot ("Pirates of Aragua"), as the on-the-ground organizer of the kidnapping. Text messages recovered by investigators allegedly show Gámez directing the operation on instructions from senior gang leadership outside Chile ("The order comes from above and they are putting their trust in me," Gámez wrote to his crew, per court documents cited by ProPublica). Gámez was later arrested in the United States, and on June 30, 2026, a U.S. magistrate judge in the Central District of California (Charles F. Eick) granted a Certification of Extraditability under 18 U.S.C. § 3184 — an extradition finding, not a U.S. or Chilean criminal conviction or indictment on the Ojeda matter. Per ProPublica, Gámez consented to extradition to face the Chilean charges; roughly 20 suspects were reportedly being prepared for trial in Santiago as of July 2026. Gámez has denied any role in Ojeda's death or any Tren de Aragua membership, telling ProPublica by phone and email from a Los Angeles federal jail that he worked in a restaurant and as a delivery driver, and that he has "no connections to Cabello." He said he is being made a "fall guy for political reasons" and that both the Chilean and U.S. governments have used the case to target Venezuelan migrants generally.

**The Cabello allegation — testimony, not adjudication.** Per ProPublica's review of the Chilean investigative file, three cooperating witnesses — accused participants in the case — testified that Cabello personally ordered the operation. One admitted kidnapper is quoted: "Diosdado Cabello, quien es un político venezolano, dio la instrucción de hacer el secuestro" ("Diosdado Cabello, who is a Venezuelan politician, gave the instruction to carry out the kidnapping"). That witness also alleged Cabello paid Héctor Rusthenford Guerrero Flores, then Tren de Aragua's top leader, to arrange the operation. A second gang-member witness testified the killing was "ordenado por el Gobierno de Venezuela, planificado por los líderes del Tren de Aragua ... y ejecutado por los miembros de la banda que estaban en Chile" ("ordered by the Government of Venezuela, planned by the leaders of Tren de Aragua ... and carried out by gang members who were in Chile"), with funding "paid by the government." **ProPublica's own reporting states explicitly that Chilean investigators lack direct documentary or communications evidence — intercepted calls, written orders — tying Cabello personally to the gang bosses.** The case against Cabello, as reported, is testimony-based, not documentary. No U.S., Chilean, or ICC charging document naming Cabello in the Ojeda matter was located in this research pass. Cabello has publicly denied involvement, telling his Venezuelan television audience in 2024: "Venezuela has nothing to do with this kidnapping. Nothing. Resolve your problems there, in Chile."

**The ICC complaint — raised, not separately docketed against Cabello.** In 2025, Chile raised the Ojeda case with the International Criminal Court; Reuters reported Chilean officials met with ICC representatives in March 2025 (headline: "Chile meets ICC officials over Tren de Aragua-linked murder of Venezuelan dissident"). ProPublica's July 2026 story characterizes this as sitting at a "preliminary-examination stage." **Checked directly against the ICC's own docket (2026-08-28 corroboration pass, via Wayback Machine archive of icc-cpi.int, current fetch blocked by Cloudflare):** neither of the two ICC situations concerning Venezuela names Ojeda or Cabello. **Venezuela I** (ICC-02/18, referred by a group of states including Chile in 2018, covering alleged crimes against humanity since 2014) has been a **formal investigation — not a preliminary examination — since November 3, 2021**, when the Prosecutor concluded the preliminary phase and moved to open an investigation; it remains active, most recently reflected in a snapshot dated August 6, 2026. **Venezuela II** (ICC-01/20, Venezuela's own 2020 self-referral over U.S. sanctions) is unrelated to Ojeda and was **closed March 12, 2026** with a finding of no reasonable basis to proceed. Chile's Ojeda-specific complaint does not appear as its own separately named ICC matter in any public docket located in this pass. **The precise procedural relationship between Chile's Ojeda referral and the Venezuela I investigation is unresolved** — ProPublica's "preliminary examination" characterization does not match Venezuela I's actual status as a live investigation, and no ICC document naming Ojeda or Cabello was found. This entry states what the ICC's own site shows rather than repeating ProPublica's framing as settled. Former officials told ProPublica that the June 2026 killing of Guerrero (Tren de Aragua's alleged top leader, in a U.S. strike — see [[cabello-diosdado]] for sourcing on that strike, which rests on the administration's own announcement) will make it harder for Chilean investigators to further develop the case against Cabello, since Guerrero was the alleged intermediary in witness accounts.

**The Donovan-Cabello meeting — independently corroborated.** ProPublica reported that SOUTHCOM commander Gen. Francis Donovan met with Cabello in Venezuela around earthquake-relief coordination. **Checked 2026-08-28**: Latin Times (published July 7, 2026, three days before ProPublica's story) independently reported the same meeting, citing Venezuela's own presidential press office and state broadcaster VTV — Donovan met acting President Delcy Rodríguez, Cabello, Defense Minister Gustavo González López and others in the wake of the June 24, 2026 La Guaira earthquakes. Miami Herald covered the same meeting the same day under the headline "U.S. handshakes with Cabello sparks outrage across Venezuelan social media." Both are independent of ProPublica. A separate, earlier Donovan-Venezuela visit (Feb 18-19, 2026, days after Maduro's Jan 3, 2026 capture, focused on counter-narcotics and immigration) was independently reported by Argus Media, Reuters, AP, France 24, and Fox News; Argus reported Cabello was "also involved in meetings, some sources said." These are two distinct Donovan-Cabello contact events.

**No new charge or extradition request naming Cabello.** Checked 2026-08-28: targeted searches (English and Spanish, including Chilean formal-charge terminology — "formalización," "orden de captura," "extraditar," "imputa") found no report of any charge or extradition request naming Cabello specifically, through August 2026. The most recent substantive reporting on the Cabello allegation remains ProPublica's July 10, 2026 story. This is a bounded negative across the corpora searched (Google News RSS index, general web), not a claim that no such Chilean court document exists — Chile's own case-lookup portal (oficinajudicialvirtual.pjud.cl) is CAPTCHA-gated and was not checkable by this research pass.

**The family.** Ojeda's family lawyer, Juan Carlos Manríquez, told ProPublica that Ojeda and his wife had discussed and rehearsed what to do "if there was a knock on the door," and "had agreed to protect their son at all costs by not offering any resistance." Ojeda's brother, Javier Ojeda, said on the record: "Diosdado Cabello is the person we want punished." Family members and other Venezuelan dissidents have said they hope the Santiago trial will demonstrate that the Venezuelan regime, like other authoritarian governments, used organized crime to send a message to opponents at home and abroad.

## Why This Event Matters

This case is the evidentiary core cited for the "Tren de Aragua as a Venezuelan-state proxy" characterization — the predicate underlying the Alien Enemies Act invocation against Tren de Aragua (Proclamation 10903, March 14, 2025; see [[2025-03-14--alien-enemies-act-tren-de-aragua-proclamation-10903]]). ProPublica's own reporting draws this connection directly: the administration designated Tren de Aragua a terrorist organization and characterized it as an instrument Maduro used to "invade" the United States, even as "some law enforcement officials say the administration exaggerated the threat to justify mass deportations." The Ojeda case is the most substantial evidence anywhere in this corpus for a genuine link between Tren de Aragua and Venezuelan-state direction — but it is witness testimony from an active foreign prosecution, not a documentary record, and not an adjudicated fact in any court, U.S. or Chilean or international. Treating testimony as settled fact would overstate the AEA's evidentiary predicate in exactly the direction the administration's critics allege it already has been overstated.

A second, separate structural point (documented at [[cabello-diosdado]]): the same administration citing Tren de Aragua's alleged state sponsorship to justify wartime-detention authority over Venezuelan deportees has continued direct diplomatic engagement with Cabello — the official most credibly implicated, per this active foreign investigation, in ordering that same network's violence — without publicly addressing the allegation. The Department of Justice declined to comment on any ongoing investigations in response to ProPublica's questions; the White House referred questions to DOJ. This is a documented inconsistency in application, not evidence that either the deportation theory or the diplomatic engagement is, on its own terms, false.

## Broader Context

Ojeda's killing sits within a broader pattern documented in this corpus of authoritarian regimes using transnational criminal networks to reach dissidents abroad — a mechanism distinct from, but functionally related to, the U.S. administration's own use of designated "terrorist" status to expand executive removal authority (see [[2025-01-20--cartels-designated-terrorists]] and [[1942-02-19--executive-order-9066-fdr-wartime-power-template]] for the domestic wartime-authority precedent Proclamation 10903 invokes). Chile's then-president, Gabriel Boric, made the ICC referral and publicly described relations with Venezuela in stark terms; the case remained a live diplomatic irritant through the January 2026 U.S. capture of Maduro (see [[2026-01-03--us-military-invades-venezuela-captures-maduro]]), after which the Trump administration restructured its relationship with the Venezuelan successor government while leaving Cabello in his ministerial post.
