NPR reported on August 20, 2026 that a coalition of privacy and civil rights groups had "filed a consumer complaint Wednesday" with the Maryland Attorney General, asking the office "to investigate specific companies and take enforcement action against them". The complaint alleges that Penlink, Thomson Reuters, Motorola, Insight LPR, LexisNexis, Flock Safety and others "are collecting and selling personal information and location data on Maryland residents to law enforcement customers in violation of state privacy law". NPR writes that Penlink and Thomson Reuters "denied the allegations and told NPR they were in compliance with the law"; Penlink told NPR that "The allegations against Penlink in the complaint are false, as Penlink does not process or sell precise location data of Marylanders in accordance with Maryland privacy law", and Thomson Reuters said "We are confident that we are in compliance with all applicable laws and regulations governing our business and operations." The complaint asks for an investigation rather than relief in court, which makes it a test of whether a state data-broker statute carries enforcement weight against federal customers.

NPR reports that "State law also bars data brokers from selling information to agencies that enforce immigration law, unless the law requires it or they receive a warrant." That is NPR's account of Maryland's statute, and the complaint "argues that the named companies are violating the law by selling location data, or by selling to ICE, or — in some cases — both." NPR adds that "Penlink sells cellphone location data through its Webloc program". About a second Penlink product, NPR reports that Penlink told it PLX is a "warrant-based digital evidence platform" that helps law enforcement organize geolocation data, but does not provide it. The award record for ICE's PLX software is in [[2026-04-29--ice-task-force-penlink-plx-sole-source-8a-chenega-award]]: a software and support purchase through a reseller, with no source we read showing interception without a warrant.

The legal question behind the purchases is open. Whether a government purchase from a data broker is a Fourth Amendment search has not been decided by the Supreme Court; the Miller and Carpenter background is in [[1976-04-21--united-states-v-miller-bank-records-third-party-doctrine]] and [[fourth-amendment-and-location-data-law]], and the FBI's side is in [[2026-03-18--patel-fbi-confirms-warrantless-purchase-commercial-data]]. States are acting too: NPR says "New Jersey, Virginia, Oregon and Connecticut also recently banned the sale of some categories of sensitive data, including geolocation data." New Jersey's A5328, in the reprint of the bill read here, provides that "In no case shall a data broker or data collector sell or license sensitive data to any other individual or entity", and its definition of sensitive data includes "precise geolocation data". That is a ban on sale. The reprint has no warrant requirement and no clause about law enforcement: the words warrant and law enforcement do not appear in it. We did not find the chaptered law, so the text as signed is not read here; the signing is in [[2026-06-30--new-jersey-enacts-costliest-data-broker-law-a5328]]. NPR also notes that "The Maryland Online Data Privacy Act passed in 2024". Other state actions in the timeline: [[2026-06-16--vermont-data-privacy-online-surveillance-act-broker-deletion-rights]], [[2026-05-07--ftc-bars-kochava-selling-sensitive-location-data-settlement]].

## Correction (2026-11-17)

An earlier version of this entry said that New Jersey enacted a warrant requirement for geolocation sales to law enforcement. The bill text has no such requirement. It bans selling sensitive data, and the line is removed. A line in the earlier version, a no-bid PLX award for warrantless real-time interception, is removed. The award record's description reads THIS AWARD IS FOR PENLINK PLX INVESTIGATIVE SOFTWARE SUITE AND SUPPORT SERVICES FOR HOMELAND SECURITY TASK FORCE. and interception without a warrant is in no source we read. The link now points to the rebuilt entry. Lines that treated the third-party doctrine as deciding whether a purchase needs a warrant are removed. Lines on Connecticut and Vermont law and the Kochava settlement had no source in this entry and are reduced to links. The earlier version also dated the filing August 20. NPR reports the complaint was filed Wednesday, before the Thursday date of its own report, so this entry carries the date of NPR's report and not the filing date. The status is now reported, not confirmed.
