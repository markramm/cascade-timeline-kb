In the early evening of Friday, December 15, 2000, with Christmas recess only hours away and the presidential election still unresolved, the U.S. Senate rushes to pass an essential 11,000-page government reauthorization bill. Senator Phil Gramm of Texas inserts a complex 262-page amendment - the Commodity Futures Modernization Act - at the last minute, in what one legal textbook would later call "a stunning departure from normal legislative practice."

The 262-page CFMA buried within the massive omnibus spending bill specifies that the Commodity Exchange Act does not apply to contracts between "eligible contract participants" in financial products ("excluded commodities"), and lets eligible contract participants trade over-the-counter derivatives in non-agricultural, non-financial commodities such as metals and energy without CFTC regulation, except for certain anti-fraud and anti-manipulation provisions, per the Congressional Research Service. The timing and method of insertion make meaningful congressional review impossible - members must vote on the entire government funding package without time to analyze the derivatives provisions. This legislative maneuver ensures passage of legislation that would never survive normal committee scrutiny or public debate.

The bill passes and is signed by President Clinton on December 21, 2000, just as he leaves office. The CFMA represents the culmination of Wall Street's decades-long campaign to eliminate derivatives oversight, incorporating recommendations from the President's Working Group that explicitly rejected Brooksley Born's warnings. Gramm's wife Wendy had granted Enron its initial derivatives exemption in 1993 and joined Enron's board five weeks later; Phil Gramm now completes the deregulatory project by securing the Act's exclusions for over-the-counter derivatives. The "lame duck" insertion of the CFMA into a must-pass spending bill exemplifies legislative corruption - using procedural manipulation to enact policies that benefit specific corporate interests while evading democratic accountability.

## Correction (2026-11-16)

This entry said the Act banned all derivatives regulation and prohibited federal and state regulation of over-the-counter derivatives. The Congressional Research Service describes narrower terms: the Act excluded certain contracts between eligible contract participants from the Commodity Exchange Act, left anti-fraud and anti-manipulation provisions applying to energy and metals contracts, and left the CFTC with jurisdiction over exchange-traded futures. The rest of this entry (the page counts, the insertion on December 15 and the surrounding account) was not re-read at source in this pass.
