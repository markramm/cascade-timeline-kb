In a March 25, 2026 post, the Pinelands Preservation Alliance directed New Jersey residents to "**Ask your town council to adopt an ordinance explicitly prohibiting data centers as a permitted use in your municipality—like Pemberton Township already did**," and linked a **model ordinance** it had published in February 2026. The document is a four-page, fill-in-the-blank template carrying bracketed placeholders throughout — "[BOROUGH/TOWNSHIP/TOWN/CITY] OF […..]", "ORDINANCE #", "Chapter …., '….', Article …" — ready for a municipal clerk to complete.

Its content is a complete drafting kit. The recital block supplies the factual predicate: that large data centers "can use as much as 3 to 5 million gallons of water in a single day between liquid cooling and water consumed to generate the energy that powers them"; that some "require 300 MW of electricity, which is roughly equivalent to the output of a small modular nuclear reactor"; that consumer energy costs across most of the state rose "more than twenty percent (20%)" in a year. It asserts the legal ground — "municipal regulations designed for the preservation of the environment and the protection of ecological values are a well-recognized, legitimate and proper exercise of municipality authority." It then supplies a DATA CENTER definition, a section designating all data centers a non-permitted use in every zone, and the full Municipal Land Use Law procedural sequence a challenged ordinance needs to survive: ten-day notice to the county planning board and adjoining municipalities under N.J.S.A. 40:55D-15, referral to the planning board for a 35-day master-plan-consistency report under N.J.S.A. 40:55D-26 and 40:55D-64, and filing of the adopted ordinance under N.J.S.A. 40:55D-16. Those are precisely the procedural steps whose omission the developer suits against Andover and Montgomery allege.

**This is the strongest documented instance of supply-side coordination in the 2026 data-center moratorium wave.** The corpus's working finding has been that the wave produces convergent outcomes through independent local decisions rather than a shared script — a reading tested and confirmed in Florida, where DeSoto, Sarasota, and North Port each designed to local procedural facts with no shared consultants or model text. New Jersey is the exception the record now names: an advocacy organization publishing template ordinance language and actively promoting its adoption.

**But the template's circulation is not evidence that towns are using it.** A direct textual comparison against Jackson Township's Ordinance 2026-25 — adopted August 25, 2026, six months after the model was published, in a township partly within the Pinelands Area — finds effectively nothing in common: 2 shared 8-gram sequences out of 1,376 in the model and 744 in Jackson's text, a Jaccard similarity of 0.0009, and both of those shared strings are generic New Jersey enacting boilerplate ("now therefore be it ordained by the mayor"). None of the model's six most distinctive phrases appears in Jackson's ordinance, and none of Jackson's six appears in the model. The two even make different structural choices: the model designates data centers a "non-permitted use," while Jackson creates an affirmative "Prohibited Use" section and adds a warehouse-excluding definition the model does not contain. Jackson's municipal attorneys drafted their own text.

The most probative test could not be run. **Pemberton Township's Ordinance 10-2026 — adopted February 20, 2026, the first data-center ban in New Jersey and the exemplar the Alliance itself points to — is a scanned image PDF with no extractable text layer**, so whether the cited model town matches the model remains open.

The honest reading: **a model ordinance exists and is being promoted; demand-side coordination is real and organized; drafting appears to remain local.** The Alliance's own account of the mechanism is diffusion rather than distribution — its post describes residents "playing zoning 'whack-a-mole' as projects pop up across the state" and towns "still catching up" to a rapidly emerging issue. The same post supplies the origin story for the Monroe litigation: residents there mobilized against a warehouse "that residents worried was actually a data center in disguise," pointing to Vineland, "where a project originally approved and built as a golf course ultimately became the site of a massive data center." That suspicion was subsequently validated — the developer holding Monroe's warehouse approvals applied to convert one building to a data center, and then sued the township over the ban that followed ([[2026-06-05--hexa-builders-sues-monroe-nj-data-center-ban-first-amendment-medium-of-expression]]).
