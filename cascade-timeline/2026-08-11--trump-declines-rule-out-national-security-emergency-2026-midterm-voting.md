In an interview with conservative commentator Wayne Allyn Root released August 11, 2026, President Trump declined to rule out declaring a national security emergency to impose federal voting rules on the November midterms. Root proposed the mechanism himself and in detail: if the Senate fails to pass the SAVE America Act, the president could declare a national emergency and obtain photo-ID requirements, proof-of-citizenship voter registration, and restrictions on mail-in ballots without legislation. "If you do this in the next month," Root told him, "we will get photo ID, proof of citizenship and a limit to mail-in ballots, and you don't even need the Senate's help." Root further asserted that such a declaration "can't [be] challenge[d]" and "can only be overturned by a two-thirds vote by both houses of Congress" — a characterization of emergency-powers law advanced by Root, not by the administration and not established as a legal matter. Trump cut him off and answered: "Let me just say that stranger things have happened, OK? I'll leave it at that." The exchange is documented across CNN, The Hill, and Democracy Docket, and the quotation is consistent in all three.

The constitutional position is not close, and the value of this entry is the documented statement rather than any prediction of what follows from it. Election-law scholars and election administrators say the president holds no constitutional authority to unilaterally set the rules by which states run federal elections: the Elections Clause gives states primary power over the time, place, and manner of election administration, with Congress — not the executive — holding the power to make or alter those regulations. Federal courts have already so held against this administration in this cycle, blocking the March 2025 elections executive order's proof-of-citizenship and ballot-deadline provisions. The National Emergencies Act confers no power over election administration, and the two-thirds figure Root cited describes only the margin needed to override a presidential veto of a termination resolution, not a bar on judicial review.

The statement is the second documented instance of the emergency instrument being aimed at election administration rather than an isolated remark. In February 2026 a draft executive order circulated that would have invoked emergency authority over election procedures ([[2026-02-26--trump-draft-emergency-election-executive-order]]) — the mechanism existed on paper months before Trump was asked about it on the record. What the August exchange adds is that the president, asked directly whether he would use it, chose not to say no.
