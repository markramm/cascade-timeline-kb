Republican-controlled legislatures in six states — Texas, Missouri, North Carolina, Ohio, Florida, and Tennessee — were scheduling August 2026 special sessions for mid-decade partisan redistricting ahead of the November midterms, in the direct wake of the Supreme Court's 6-3 ruling in Louisiana v. Callais rendering Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act effectively inoperable ([[2026-04-29--scotus-callais-guts-section-2-vra-reworks-gingles-standard]]). The Brennan Center and the Campaign Legal Center both described the sequence as the final dismantling of the VRA rather than a further weakening of it. Mid-decade redistricting breaks the once-per-decade norm that has governed congressional map-drawing since the census cycle was established; six states doing it in the same month, in the same summer as the ruling that removed the constraint, is not six independent decisions.

The mechanism worth naming is the interval. Callais was decided in April; the Court ordered it into immediate effect in May, overriding roughly 100,000 already-cast ballots ([[2026-05-04--scotus-callais-vra-section-2-immediate-effect-order-overrides-100k-ballots]]); Southern states began racing to redraw maps eliminating majority-Black districts in June ([[2026-06-12--vra-gutted-southern-states-race-to-redraw-maps-eliminating-majority-black-districts]]); and by August the redraw had become a scheduled legislative calendar item in six states at once. Under the preclearance regime that Shelby County dismantled and Section 2 litigation had partially substituted for, each of these maps would have faced federal review before taking effect. With Section 2 inoperable, the maps take effect and the challenge, if any, comes after the election they decide.

Texas is the template the others are following, and the corpus has it documented end to end: Trump demanding the redraw in July 2025 ([[2025-07-15--trump-demands-texas-redistricting]]), the legislature passing it without Democrats present ([[2025-07-25--texas-passes-redistricting-without-democrats]]), and the Supreme Court reinstating the map in December 2025 over a district court's findings of racial gerrymandering ([[2025-12-04--supreme-court-reinstates-texas-mid-decade-redistricting-map]]). The relevant lesson from that sequence was not that the map survived but that it survived fast enough to be used. The corpus also records the counter-case — Alabama's court injunction and South Carolina's Senate rejection in May ([[2026-05-26--trump-mid-decade-redistricting-double-setback-alabama-court-injunction-south-carolina-senate-rejection]]) — which is why the distinction between states that announced special sessions and states that actually convened and passed maps is the scoreboard to watch through September, not the count of announcements.
