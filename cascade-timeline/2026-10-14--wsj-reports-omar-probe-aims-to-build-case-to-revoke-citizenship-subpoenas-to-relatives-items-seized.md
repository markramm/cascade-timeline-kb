The Wall Street Journal reported on Wednesday, Oct. 14 (the day Mediaite and Townhall relayed it; the WSJ page is paywalled and was not read directly), that the federal investigation of Rep. Ilhan Omar is aimed at building a case to revoke her citizenship and deport her. The WSJ's own text is not in hand. Everything below is the WSJ account as relayed by Colorado Politics, USA TODAY, Mediaite and Townhall, plus the Washington Post and MS NOW, all resting on anonymous sources. MPR News states it "has not independently confirmed" the WSJ's account of the aim. The Justice Department has not publicly confirmed it; DOJ and DHS did not respond to Colorado Politics.

What is new relative to the Oct. 12 search entry: (1) the stated aim, denaturalization and deportation, rather than only "immigration paperwork"; (2) the subpoenas, reported as "more than a dozen," including to relatives, with the New York Times (relayed, not read) naming her sister Sahra Noor; (3) the reported inventory of items taken from the home: cellphone, notebooks, personal computers and passport. The Washington Post, which does not itself say what was taken, reports that the warrant listed potential charges of conspiracy, immigration fraud and false statements, and that the question under examination is whether Omar or her father, Nur Omar Mohamed, intentionally misstated her birth date so that she qualified for derivative citizenship at 17 (she became a citizen in 2000). Investigators have also examined whether her father omitted military history when seeking status; he died in 2020. A former immigration official told the Post that HSI had declined to pursue similar allegations years earlier.

Dates and their kinds: the search was Monday, Oct. 12 (a USA TODAY photo caption says Oct. 9, contradicting its own body text and the ABC/NPR coverage in the Oct. 12 entry; treat the caption as an error). Subpoena issuance dates are not established; one summary says "beginning last Friday" (Oct. 9), which is a single secondary relay. The WSJ report date, Oct. 14, is a press-pickup date, not a government act.

Unresolved discrepancy: the count of denaturalization suits is reported as "at least 163" in one summary and "more than 200 this term" in a Mediaite relay of WSJ; neither is a court or DOJ tally, and this entry adopts neither. Mechanism context: revocation requires a federal judge, in a civil case or by criminal conviction.
