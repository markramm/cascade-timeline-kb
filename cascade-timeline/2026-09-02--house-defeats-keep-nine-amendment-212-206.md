The House voted 212-206 on September 2, 2026 against the "Keep Nine Amendment," a joint resolution proposed by Rep. Andy Biggs (R-AZ) that would have required the Supreme Court to "be composed of nine justices." A constitutional amendment needs a two-thirds majority in both chambers plus ratification by three-fourths of the states; 212 votes is not close to that threshold, and the outcome was not in doubt when the vote was scheduled.

The breakdown: 211 Republicans and Rep. Don Davis (D-NC) voted in favor; 205 Democrats and Rep. Kevin Kiley (I-CA) voted against. The near-party-line split is the point of the exercise. Speaker Mike Johnson brought a measure to the floor that could not pass in order to put members on record before the November midterms — a procedural vote whose product is a roster, not a law.

What makes it worth recording is the direction of travel it documents. An amendment to *freeze* the Court's size is a defensive move, and it was brought because expansion has stopped being a fringe position: openness within the Democratic caucus to adding four seats has grown through a year in which the Court overturned *Humphrey's Executor* in *Trump v. Slaughter*, reworked the *Gingles* standard in *Callais*, and repeatedly granted emergency relief on the shadow docket. Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries has pledged substantial Court reform as a priority if Democrats take the chamber in November.

Both parties are now treating the Court's composition as an object of ordinary politics rather than a settled constraint on it. The vote changed nothing about the Court and everything about what is sayable in public regarding its size.
