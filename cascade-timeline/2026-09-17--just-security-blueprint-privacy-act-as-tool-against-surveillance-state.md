**Just Security** published **"Reinvention Blueprint No. 3"** on **September 17, 2026**, arguing that the **Privacy Act of 1974** — largely unenforced under administrations of both parties — is the most powerful legal instrument currently available for dismantling the domestic surveillance architecture built since 2025. The piece contends that the administration has eroded the inter-agency data-silo distinctions the Act was written to enforce, and that rigorous enforcement by Democratic-led state attorneys general and private litigants could impose meaningful constraints **without new legislation**. The Blueprint series is explicitly aimed at post-2026 reform governance planning.

This is an argument rather than an occurrence, and it earns a timeline slot as a **legal-strategy marker** — the point at which a specific statutory theory enters the reform conversation with an institutional author behind it. The claim it rests on is one the corpus has documented independently: the 1974 Act's operative premise is that agencies hold data in separate systems of records and may not freely merge them, and the 2025 data-centralization order ([[2025-08-01--trump-state-data-centralization-surveillance-order]]) together with the DOGE cross-agency access NPR reported ([[2026-01-23--npr-reveals-doge-privacy-act-violations]]) describe exactly the merger the statute contemplates as the harm. The "no new legislation" framing is the load-bearing part: every other reform vehicle on this beat — the Government Surveillance Reform Act ([[2026-03-12--wyden-lee-introduce-government-surveillance-reform-act-warrant-for-data-broker-purchases]]), the SAFE Act — requires a Congress willing to pass it, while the Privacy Act requires only a plaintiff and a court. Whether state AGs take it up is the testable prediction this entry exists to be checked against.
