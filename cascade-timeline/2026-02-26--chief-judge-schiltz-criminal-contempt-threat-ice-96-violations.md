On February 26, 2026, Chief Judge Patrick J. Schiltz of the District of Minnesota filed a supplemental order in a habeas case. It ends with a conditional warning to ICE: “This Court will continue to do whatever is required to protect the rule of law, including, if necessary, moving to the use of criminal contempt. One way or another, ICE will comply with this Court’s orders.” The order does not hold anyone in criminal contempt and does not order an arrest. It says what the court will do if ICE keeps violating orders. Schiltz wrote that the court had had to resort to using “the threat of civil contempt” and was “not aware of another occasion in the history of the United States in which a federal court has had to threaten contempt” again and again to force the government to comply with court orders.

The order answers a February 9 email from U.S. Attorney Daniel N. Rosen, who had reviewed a sample of the cases on the court’s January 28 list and wrote that the list was “far beyond the pale of accuracy.” Schiltz had the judges and his law clerks recheck it. The January 28 order’s appendix had identified 96 orders ICE violated in 74 cases. The recheck found mistakes “that cut both ways,” and the bottom line was that ICE violated 97 orders in 66 of those cases. A new Appendix B listed 113 further orders violated in 77 further cases, most of the violations after January 28. Schiltz turned Rosen’s phrase back on ICE: “ICE’s continued violation of the orders of this Court” is what is beyond the pale. The January 28 order had put the January count in these terms: ICE has likely violated “more court orders in January 2026 than some federal agencies have violated in their entire existence.”

The order also puts a number on the surge. Schiltz wrote that what the Justice Department’s lawyers did not deserve was “the Administration sending 3000 ICE agents to Minnesota to detain people without making any provision for handling the hundreds of lawsuits that were sure to follow.” The Associated Press reported Rosen’s own count of prosecutors: 36 assistant U.S. attorneys, down from 47 on his first day.
