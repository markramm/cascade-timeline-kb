On June 22, 2018, the Supreme Court decided *Carpenter v. United States*. Chief Justice Roberts wrote the opinion of the Court, in which Justices Ginsburg, Breyer, Sotomayor and Kagan joined. Justice Kennedy dissented, joined by Justices Thomas and Alito; Justices Thomas, Alito and Gorsuch each wrote a separate dissent. The case came from the Sixth Circuit; the Court reversed and remanded.

The records were compelled, not bought. Prosecutors obtained orders under the Stored Communications Act directing carriers to disclose Timothy Carpenter's cell-site records, and the Government ended up with 12,898 location points, including 127 days of records from one carrier. The Court held: "we hold that an individual maintains a legitimate expectation of privacy in the record of his physical movements as captured through CSLI." Its narrowest statement of the holding is in a footnote: "accessing seven days of CSLI constitutes a Fourth Amendment search." The Court declined to say whether a shorter period would be one: "we need not decide whether there is a limited period for which the Government may obtain an individual’s historical CSLI free from Fourth Amendment scrutiny".

On the Government's route to the records, the Court wrote that "the Government must generally obtain a warrant supported by probable cause before acquiring such records" and that "an order issued under Section 2703(d) of the Act is not a permissible mechanism for accessing historical cell-site records". It allowed for emergencies: "case-specific exceptions may support a warrantless search" in some circumstances.

**What the Court said it was not deciding.** "We do not express a view on matters not before us: real-time CSLI" and, in the same sentence, tower dumps. Then: "We do not disturb the application of Smith and Miller or call into question conventional surveillance techniques and tools, such as security cameras." And: "Nor do we address other business records that might incidentally reveal location information." And: "our opinion does not consider other collection techniques involving foreign affairs or national security". All of this is one paragraph near the end of the Court's analysis. Declining to address a category leaves it open; the opinion does not hold that the third-party doctrine governs it. In describing how carriers use the data, the opinion notes that "wireless carriers often sell aggregated location records to data brokers, without individual identifying information of the sort at issue here". It does not discuss government purchases from brokers.

**Good faith.** The Court did not apply the good-faith exception to the exclusionary rule: the phrase does not appear anywhere in the slip opinion, which reversed and remanded. The Sixth Circuit's decision on remand was not read for this entry.

**Sentence.** The opinion says Carpenter was "sentenced to more than 100 years in prison".

**The dissents,** each in the dissenting Justice's own words. Kennedy: "This case should be resolved by interpreting accepted property principles as the baseline for reasonable expectations of privacy." Thomas: "Because the Katz test is a failed experiment, this Court is dutybound to reconsider it." Alito: "the Court allows a defendant to object to the search of a third party’s property". Gorsuch: "I cannot help but conclude—reluctantly—that Mr. Carpenter forfeited perhaps his most promising line of argument."

**Reading across.** The opinion's own reservations mark the boundary for any later claim that *Carpenter* requires a warrant for some other kind of data: what it decided was historical cell-site records compelled from carriers. The 2026 decision in *Chatrie* applied the reasonable-expectation reasoning to location data held by Google; see [[2026-06-29--scotus-chatrie-location-data-fourth-amendment-geofence-warrant]].
