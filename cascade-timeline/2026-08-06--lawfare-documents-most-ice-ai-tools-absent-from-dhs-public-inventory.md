A **Lawfare** analysis published August 6, 2026 documents the gap between what DHS deploys and what
DHS discloses. By Lawfare's count, **CBP operates approximately 75 AI tools and ICE operates 42 —
but only 23 of ICE's appeared in DHS's public AI Use Case Inventory.** The public inventory is the
mechanism through which federal AI deployment is supposed to become reviewable; a majority of ICE's
systems sit outside it.

The structural reading Lawfare offers is that immigration enforcement functions as the proving
ground for executive AI governance: it is where automated decisions bear on liberty, where they move
faster than any oversight mechanism reviewing them, and where the accountability framework for an
AI-driven error remains undefined. The inventory gap is not a paperwork lag — it is what determines
which systems can be asked about at all.

The Lawfare piece appears alongside **FedScoop** reporting on a **CBP contract with Clearview AI**
awarded **February 2026** — roughly **$225,000 for 15 licenses** — at the **National Targeting
Center** ([[2026-02-11--cbp-clearview-ai-facial-recognition-contract]]). That placement extends
biometric identification from border crossings to anyone screened against the NTC watchlist
database. It sits in a documented sequence: ICE's largest Clearview contract alongside DHS deleting
its oversight policy ([[2026-06-20--ice-largest-clearview-ai-contract-dhs-deletes-oversight-policy]]),
Clearview's FedRAMP authorization covering all travelers
([[2026-07-08--clearview-fedramp-cbp-facial-recognition-all-travelers]]), and the LexisNexis/Palantir
bulk facial-recognition contract ([[2026-08-10--ice-lexisnexis-palantir-bulk-facial-recognition-contract]]).

**Attribution note.** The tool counts (75 CBP, 42 ICE, 23 inventoried) are **Lawfare's figures**,
recorded here as Lawfare's characterization rather than as primary-source counts. The event date is
the Lawfare publication date; the Clearview contract itself dates to February 2026.
