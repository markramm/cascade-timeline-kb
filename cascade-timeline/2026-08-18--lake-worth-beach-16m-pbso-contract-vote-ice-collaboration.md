Lake Worth Beach's city commission scheduled an August 18, 2026 vote on a 10-year contract extension with the Palm Beach County Sheriff's Office worth roughly $16 million per year, which immigration advocates argue functions as a vehicle for ICE collaboration. At an August 13 press conference, advocates cited the deportation of Olga Perez, a community interpreter who left four U.S.-citizen children behind, as a direct consequence of existing PBSO-ICE cooperation. Commissioner Christopher McVoy publicly opposed the contract structure. Palm Beach County ranks 10th of 100 counties on the detention-pipeline heat index, in the 91st percentile.

The contract is the mechanism worth tracking, not the rhetoric around it. A small municipality that contracts out its entire police function to a county sheriff inherits that sheriff's federal entanglements wholesale, and it does so through a procurement decision rather than any vote on immigration policy. Residents who might reject a 287(g) agreement outright are instead presented with a routine ten-year law-enforcement services renewal. That indirection is what makes these agreements durable where explicit ICE cooperation is contested — the corpus documents the explicit fights (Cumberland County ME terminating its IGSA, 2026-04-22--cumberland-county-me-terminates-ice-igsa; New York sheriffs defying state 287(g) termination orders, 2026-08-14--nassau-county-ny-defies-287g-termination-deadline) alongside the quieter contractual path, backed by a $2 billion funding pipeline for local police deputization (2026-05-07--ice-287g-funding-pipeline-2-billion-local-police-deputization). Florida offers no state-level counterweight, which leaves the municipal contract as the only available veto point.
