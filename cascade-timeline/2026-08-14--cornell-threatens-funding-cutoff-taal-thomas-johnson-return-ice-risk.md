The Intercept reported on August 14, 2026 that Cornell University reversed an earlier pledge to protect two graduate students, Momodou Taal and Amandla Thomas-Johnson, and threatened to terminate their funding unless they returned to the United States — where the administration has targeted both for deportation over participation in pro-Palestine protests. Cornell had previously told both students it would help them avoid immigration detention. Both are currently abroad and outside ICE's reach; the funding threat is the mechanism that would compel their return.

The university is not being compelled to deport anyone. It is being asked, and is choosing, to apply the one form of leverage a federal agency does not have over students located outside U.S. jurisdiction — their livelihood. That converts an institution from a buffer into a delivery mechanism, which is the more durable capture: no federal action is required against Taal and Thomas-Johnson abroad so long as their own university creates the incentive to come back. Cornell had already settled with the administration for $60 million (2025-11-07--cornell-university-settlement-60-million), which establishes the prior. The academic-capture pattern is long-documented in the corpus, from CIA-funded university front foundations (1951-01-01--cia-university-funding-front-foundations-academic-capture) forward. The immediate enforcement context is the targeting of student protesters for deportation and the Houston case of Mauro Henriquez, deported to Honduras after months in detention (2026-04-09--houston-student-mauro-henriquez-deported-honduras-after-months-detention). What is new is the discovery that a protective pledge from a university is worth precisely as much as the university's willingness to absorb federal pressure — and that the pledge itself, once reversed, becomes the leverage.
