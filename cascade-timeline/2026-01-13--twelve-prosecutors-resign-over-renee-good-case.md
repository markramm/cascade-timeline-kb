At least six attorneys left the Minnesota U.S. Attorney's Office on Jan. 13, according to the Star Tribune, which reported that a majority of the office's leadership team resigned. Its reporting rests on sources familiar with the decision. The Star Tribune names four of them. Joe Thompson, who was No. 2 in the office, was its lead federal prosecutor of white-collar crime. Harry Jacobs was chief of the criminal division. Thomas Calhoun-Lopez was the lead investigator for a series of federal racketeering trials targeting Minneapolis street gangs, and Melinda Williams led the prosecution of sex trafficker Anton Lazzaro. The Star Tribune writes: "Thompson, Jacobs and Williams are the three top-ranking prosecutors in the office." Calhoun-Lopez is not among them.

The Star Tribune's sources say the departures stemmed from directives after the killing of Renee Good by ICE agent Jonathan Ross: blocking the Minnesota Bureau of Criminal Apprehension from the investigation, and a request from the Justice Department to investigate Good's widow for possible federal charges. A source also said Thompson was frustrated that the immigration surge had "eclipsed" fraud investigations. NPR's source says the investigation of the widow allegedly centers on possible ties to activist groups. CBS News reports the investigation is being treated as an assault on a federal officer, with Ross, not Good, seen as the victim of a crime. Deputy Attorney General Todd Blanche told CBS: "There is currently no basis for a criminal civil rights investigation." NOTUS reports the FBI opted to handle the investigation without coordinating with local officials, and that the BCA said it had "reluctantly withdrawn" after the FBI pulled its clearances.

Separately, leaders of the Civil Rights Division's criminal section in Washington announced their departures. NOTUS, citing MSNOW, reports at least four officials left after Assistant Attorney General Harmeet Dhillon announced the unit would not investigate the shooting of Good; CBS News counts at least six prosecutors, most of them supervisors, including the section's chief. A Justice Department official told CBS the leaders gave notice under an early retirement program and sought it "well before the events in Minnesota." CBS's sources say the departures were not solely driven by Minneapolis, though "The lack of an investigation in Minneapolis was a breaking point for some of them." CBS adds that the criminal section had lost fewer attorneys than the division's other sections, which collectively lost approximately 75% of their staff in 2025.
