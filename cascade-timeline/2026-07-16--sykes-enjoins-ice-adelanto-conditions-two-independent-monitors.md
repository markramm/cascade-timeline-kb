U.S. District Judge Sunshine Suzanne Sykes issued a preliminary injunction on July 16, 2026 ordering ICE to correct conditions at the Adelanto ICE Processing Center in San Bernardino County, California, after finding detainees likely to succeed on their Fifth Amendment claims. The order requires 24-hour access to clean drinking water, nutritious meals, free hygiene products, blankets and temperature-appropriate clothing, daily cleaning with mold removal, at least four hours of recreational yard time a day, unrestricted family visitation with physical contact during business hours, and two daily headcounts confirming each detainee's presence and condition. The government was given 14 days to produce a comprehensive medical-care and disability-accommodation plan. Most consequentially, Sykes appointed **two independent monitors for the duration of the lawsuit**, with detainees able to submit grievances in English or Spanish through a lockbox only the monitors can open — a compliance architecture that does not depend on the agency or its contractor self-reporting.

The list of ordered items is banal by design, and that is the finding: each entry names something the plaintiffs established was not reliably being provided at a facility the federal government pays GEO Group to run. Adelanto had already recorded four deaths in seven months by March 2026, a California record ([[2026-03-30--adelanto-geo-fourth-death-california-record-seven-months]]), and this corpus documents Mario Parias held roughly 300 days there with an untreated gunshot wound ([[2026-08-16--parias-shot-journalist-300-days-adelanto-untreated-wound]]). **This injunction is the predicate for the appellate ruling that followed:** DHS moved to stay it, and on August 21, 2026 a unanimous Ninth Circuit panel denied the stay, holding that contracting operations to GEO does not relieve the federal government of its own constitutional duty ([[2026-08-21--ninth-circuit-denies-dhs-stay-adelanto-conditions-injunction-lt-v-ice]]).

Adelanto is not isolated, though the chronology is looser than a single "summer 2026 wave" would suggest and is worth stating accurately. In Baltimore, U.S. District Judge Julie R. Rubin entered a preliminary injunction on **March 6, 2026** in *D.N.N. v. Liggins*, finding conditions in ICE's five courthouse holding cells at 31 Hopkins Plaza unconstitutional punishment that "woefully fail to comport with contemporary standards of decency," capping the rooms at 56 people total, requiring at least 31 square feet of personal space per detainee, daily cleaning, and a medical screening within 12 hours of entry; the population had exceeded double that cap multiple times in 2025. In Manhattan, U.S. District Judge Lewis A. Kaplan's orders over 26 Federal Plaza came **earlier still** — a temporary restraining order on August 12, 2025 requiring sleeping mats, hygiene access, medical care, and confidential attorney calls, converted to a preliminary injunction on September 18, 2025 that barred ICE from holding people in less than 50 square feet each. That case continued into 2026 over compliance disputes and reached trial in late May 2026. The rulings therefore span roughly a year, not one summer; what they share is a subject matter and a legal theory, not a date.

The fourth front is the largest facility in the system. On May 30, 2026, four detainees at Camp East Montana on the Army's Fort Bliss base in El Paso filed a putative class action in the U.S. District Court for the Western District of Texas — the first suit against the camp — alleging severe beatings and sexual harassment by guards, squalid conditions, spoiled and inadequate food, disease outbreaks, and denial of medication for HIV, cancer, and diabetes. Named plaintiff Gerald Akari Angye, a Cameroonian asylum seeker, alleges guards beat him badly enough to require hospitalization and a wheelchair, then held him in solitary confinement for 15 days: "I have already experienced torture in my home country of Cameroon and I never thought I would experience such severely violent treatment by guards here in the United States of America." ICE Director Todd Lyons and DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin are named defendants; DHS Acting Assistant Secretary Lauren Bis called the allegations "categorically false," saying "ICE has higher detention standards than most U.S. prisons that hold actual U.S. citizens." The camp, a tent encampment in the Chihuahuan Desert opened in 2025, has capacity for 5,000 and typically holds about 3,000. At least three people have died there, including Gerald Lunas Campos, whose death the El Paso County medical examiner ruled a homicide ([[2026-01-21--lunas-campos-el-paso-medical-examiner-rules-homicide-ice-camp-east-montana]]). The corpus separately documents a ProPublica account of a mental-health-crisis death there ([[2026-07-03--propublica-camp-east-montana-mental-health-crisis-death]]), an 84-page HRW and ACLU report finding Fort Bliss guards betting on detainee deaths ([[2026-07-15--hrw-aclu-fort-bliss-guards-bet-on-detainee-deaths-84-page-report]]), a GAO finding that the megajail program wasted millions ([[2026-06-19--ice-megajail-program-retreats-gao-finds-camp-east-montana-wasted-millions]]), and the no-bid Amentum contracts that run it ([[2026-03-11--amentum-services-no-bid-contract-camp-east-montana]], [[2026-07-27--ice-extends-amentum-no-bid-776m-camp-east-montana]]).
