Dougherty County, Georgia commissioners voted **unanimously on August 31, 2026** to extend the county's data-center moratorium by another **180 days** — the second extension of a freeze that began as a **45-day zoning review in July 2026**. County officials stated there is **no ceiling** on how many times they can extend it.

That last detail is what distinguishes this from an ordinary pause. A moratorium with a fixed expiry is a delay; a moratorium the governing body can renew without limit is a **standing veto exercised by inaction**. The county does not have to deny an application, write a restrictive ordinance, or defend either in court — it can simply decline to let the clock run out. Where a developer's case depends on eventually reaching a hearing, an indefinitely renewable pause is the more durable instrument, and it costs the county almost nothing to maintain.

The substantive fight is visible in what commissioners argued about: whether the moratorium language should distinguish **large-scale from small-scale** data centers. That is the question of whether the county is regulating a land use or an industry. Residents are pushing for permanent regulations on **noise, setbacks, and water use** — the three constraints that do not scale away.

Georgia is where this is concentrating. This corpus records **Walker County's** 180-day moratorium with a study committee (August 11); **Augusta** extended its own in July; **DeKalb County** has adopted a pause. A state bill, **HB1059**, the Data Center Impact Assessment and Development Moratorium Act of 2026, would move the same instrument up a level.

Read against the broader pattern this timeline tracks — Washington County MD, New Castle County DE — the local-government posture has shifted from negotiating terms to **withholding permission while the terms are written**. The moratorium is not opposition to data centers; it is a refusal to approve them under the rules that currently exist.
