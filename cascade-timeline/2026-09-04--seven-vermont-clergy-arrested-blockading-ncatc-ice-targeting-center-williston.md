**Seven faith leaders** were arrested in Williston, Vermont on **September 4, 2026** after blockading the entrances to the **National Criminal Analysis and Targeting Center (NCATC)** — described in the reporting as one of **three major facilities** that help ICE agents hone their enforcement operations. They began blocking the entrances at about **7:30 a.m.** and held them for roughly **three hours**, singing, praying, and **reading the names of people killed in ICE interactions or who died in detention**.

Those arrested: **Catherine Bock**, 78, of Burlington; **Jessica Derise**, 54, of Williston; **Rebecca Girrell**, 47, of South Burlington; **Elizabeth Gleich**, 36, of Middlebury; **Karen Johnston**, 58, of Colchester; **Earl Kooperkamp**, 69, of Bradford; and **Susan McMillan**, 65, of Colchester.

**The target is the point.** This corpus records clergy arrests at detention facilities — Broadview in November 2025, the Minneapolis airport in January 2026 — where the protest stands between agents and the people being taken. Williston is different. The NCATC holds no detainees. It is **analytical infrastructure**: the place where data becomes a target list, upstream of every field operation it generates. Blockading it is a claim about where the machinery actually lives.

That distinction matters for how this timeline reads enforcement generally. A raid is visible, local, and attributable; the targeting that produced it is none of those. The corpus documents the inputs at length — the Palantir contracts, the LexisNexis data flows, the ELITE app, the voter-file and ITIN acquisitions — and the NCATC is where those inputs are converted into names and addresses. Seven people sat down in front of that building and read the names of the dead, which is an argument about causation made physically.

Note who they are. The arrested range in age from **36 to 78**; several are ordained, including an **Episcopal archdeacon and priest**, and **Rev. Rebecca Girrell** of the United Methodist Church. This is the same constituency recorded in the 400-clergy Ash Wednesday letter of February 2026 — faith leaders acting in their institutional capacity rather than as private citizens, which is what makes the arrests newsworthy and also what the participants intend.
