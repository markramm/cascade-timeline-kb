The Department of Justice's Public Integrity Section, created after Watergate to prosecute public corruption, was cut to a fraction of its staff in 2025 after the Adams corruption case. NBC News reported on March 11, 2025, citing anonymous sources, that the section would keep roughly a half-dozen employees and would "no longer directly handle investigations or prosecutions." NOTUS reported on September 22, 2025 that the 36 experienced attorneys assigned full-time when Trump took office had become two (see [[2025-09-22--doj-public-integrity-section-two-lawyers-anti-corruption-collapse]]).

PBS reported that Bove had directed Sassoon to drop Adams' case as soon as "practicable." Bove's own letter to Sassoon, dated February 13, 2025, says he had directed her on February 10 to dismiss the prosecution "based on well-founded concerns regarding weaponization, election interference, and the impediments that the case has imposed on Mayor Adams' ability to govern and cooperate with federal law enforcement to keep New York City safe"; that is his description of his reason. Sassoon told the attorney general she was "confident" Adams had committed the crimes, and resigned; PBS's article is dated Feb 13, 2025, and Bove's letter says she tendered her resignation by email "At approximately 1:50 p.m." that day. In her letter to the attorney general she wrote that she attended a meeting on January 31, 2025, at which Adams' lawyers urged what she called a "quid pro quo." Adams' attorney Alex Spiro said, in NBC's report, "The idea that there was a quid pro quo is a total lie."

NPR reported that later on Thursday, Keller, the acting head of the Public Integrity Section, and Driscoll, whom NPR described as the senior-most career official leading the Criminal Division, "also resigned after being asked to take over the Adams case," according to two anonymous sources. CBS News reported, citing two sources, that on Thursday night three additional senior officials in the section resigned, "bringing the total number of resignations over the case to six." NPR's Friday story counted "Five" lawyers tied to the section among "At least seven" prosecutors who had resigned in New York and Washington.

On Friday, February 14, Hagan Scotten, an assistant U.S. attorney in the Southern District of New York, sent his resignation letter to Bove. It is dated February 14, 2025 and says: "Please consider this my resignation." In it he wrote, "I expect you will eventually find someone who is enough of a fool, or enough of a coward, to file your motion. But it was never going to be me." Those are Scotten's words about the planned motion.

NPR reported that earlier that Friday Bove held a meeting with attorneys in the Public Integrity Section "where he pressed to find a career lawyer to participate in the Adams case." NBC reported that the filing came hours after Bove and Antoinette Bacon "held a video meeting with members of the Public Integrity Section." The Justice Department moved to dismiss that night; NPR reported the motion was "signed by acting Deputy Attorney General Emil Bove, Antoinette Bacon," and Edward Sullivan, a senior litigation counsel in the section. NBC reported, from a person familiar with the matter, that Sullivan "decided to sign it to protect his colleagues." NOTUS reported that the section's remaining leaders "refused to sign off on dropping the charges," and that "a senior prosecutor on the team, Ed Sullivan, signed the court paperwork."

Judge Dale Ho dismissed the case with prejudice, CNBC reported. CNBC reported that Ho said dismissing the case without prejudice "would create the unavoidable perception that the Mayor's freedom depends on his ability to carry out the immigration enforcement priorities of the administration." CNBC quoted him on the Justice Department's "request, if granted, would leave Mayor Adams under the specter of reindictment at essentially any time, and for essentially any reason," Ho noted. CNBC also reported that Ho wrote "Everything here smacks of a bargain."

NBC reported separately that the administration had "ended a 50-year policy of keeping the Justice Department independent of the White House in criminal investigations."
