**Jefferson County, Florida** — a rural county of about 16,000 near Tallahassee with no traffic lights — built a **Flock Safety** automated license-plate-reader network without significant public process, then moved to dismantle it after community pressure, per **Bolts Magazine's** September 16, 2026 report. Bolts describes it as one of the first documented cases of a local government reversing a commercial surveillance deployment of this kind, driven by residents who regarded the infrastructure as incompatible with the character of the place they live.

This entry is `status: reported` and rests on a **single tier-2 source**. The camera count, the specific commission vote dates, and the removal timeline are not established in the material available; the claim carried here is the reversal itself, not its mechanics. A follow-up pass against Jefferson County commission minutes would firm it up and is worth doing, because the value of this case is entirely in its particulars.

Flock's growth model is what makes a rural reversal interesting out of proportion to the county's size. The networks spread through hundreds of small jurisdictions on administrative purchase authority rather than legislative votes, with retention and access policies set by the vendor rather than the buyer — which means the deployment decision is typically made where the public is not looking and the terms are made where the county has no say at all. A county that discovers its own network after the fact and unwinds it establishes that the arrangement is reversible, which is not obvious and has not been much demonstrated. It follows the August reporting on cities and states exiting ALPR contracts as Flock added privacy controls ([[2026-08-17--flock-safety-adds-privacy-controls-as-cities-states-exit-alpr-contracts]]). The opposition here came from rural residents on community-identity grounds rather than from civil-liberties organizations, which is a different coalition than this beat usually records.
