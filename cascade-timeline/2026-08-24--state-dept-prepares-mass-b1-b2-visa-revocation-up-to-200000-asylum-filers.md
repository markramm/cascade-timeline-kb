The State Department is preparing to revoke B1/B2 tourist and business visas of up to 200,000 people, according to State Department documents obtained by the Associated Press and reported August 24. The action targets B1/B2 visas issued between 2016 and 2026 whose holders subsequently filed asylum claims — what officials describe as the largest single mass visa revocation in U.S. history, though that superlative comes from officials quoted in the reporting rather than an independently established record. Deputy Secretary of State Christopher Landau framed the move as targeting "bogus asylum claims"; State Department spokesman Tommy Pigott said the agency is "coordinating with DHS to identify and revoke" the visas. Revocation would not automatically trigger deportation — holders with pending asylum cases would be recategorized but would lose business/tourism status. As of the AP's reporting, the formal announcement had not yet been made; State told reporters it was expected "in coming weeks," and no specific legal authority for the mass action had been cited in the documents or communications made available to the press.

The scale reframes a program already running for most of a year: [[2025-12-08--state-department-85000-visa-revocations]] recorded 85,000 visa revocations across all categories since January 2025 — more than double the 2024 rate, including over 8,000 student visas — with roughly half attributed to safety-threat findings (DUIs, assaults, theft) and the rest to expirations and terrorism-support allegations. A contemplated action of up to 200,000, aimed specifically at the asylum-filing population, would be more than double that already-record figure, and unlike the earlier wave it targets people for a status they voluntarily disclosed to the government — filing for asylum — rather than for a flagged offense. That specificity is the structural escalation: cross-referencing a decade of visa issuance records against asylum filings to build a revocation list turns an immigration-benefit application into the basis for punitive action against the underlying visa.

## Research note: the authority, and two figures that must not be conflated (added 2026-08-27)

**The authority is INA § 221(i) / 8 U.S.C. § 1201(i)** — broad discretionary revocation "at any time," carrying an explicit **jurisdiction-stripping clause** that bars habeas, mandamus, and APA review except in a removal proceeding where the revocation is the *sole* ground under § 1227(a)(1)(B). That is the answer to the question this entry raised: the statute does not require a finding that the holder did anything wrong, and it forecloses most routes to challenge. Spokesman Pigott separately floated a **fraud-in-procurement theory** — that obtaining a visa in order to seek asylum is itself fraud — layered on top of the discretionary ground. **The formal notice, still unissued, is the only document that will settle which theory governs.**

**§ 221(i) is used constantly for individual revocations. What is unprecedented is the categorical, population-scale application.**

### Two figures, deliberately kept apart

- **175,000** — CONFIRMED and on the record. State Department press release, 2026-08-10: more than 175,000 visas revoked. This is the **all-category running total since January 2025.**
- **200,000** — UNVERIFIED. An "up to" ceiling attributed to officials from documents AP obtained, specific to **B1/B2 holders who filed asylum claims.**

**These are different measures and conflating them produces a false number.** The 175,000 is not a subset or a precursor of the 200,000; it is a different denominator entirely.

### Does 200,000 survive checking?

Neither confirmed nor falsified, with the arithmetic reconstructed: B1/B2 issuance runs ~6.5M/year (FY2024), asylum filings peaked around 450,000/year (FY2023), and roughly a third of asylum filers entered on B1/B2 — putting the underlying pool at **500,000-700,000**, several times the claimed ceiling. So 200,000 sits inside a plausible order of magnitude but **cannot be independently reconstructed from public data.** It remains an internal estimate the press has seen and the public has not. Cite it as such or not at all.

**No named affected individual exists in public reporting** — a verified absence consistent with the pre-implementation posture, not a gap in searching.
