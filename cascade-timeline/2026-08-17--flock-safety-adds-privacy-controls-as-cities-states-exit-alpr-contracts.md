Flock Safety, whose automated license plate reader network operates in more than 6,000 communities across 49 states, announced tightened privacy and oversight controls in August 2026 as municipalities began exiting contracts and state legislatures moved on warrant requirements. Minnesota Reformer reporting on August 17 documented the community exit decisions and the legislative pushback driving them. The network was built through individual law enforcement partnerships and has been increasingly queried for immigration enforcement.

The procurement path is the reason this infrastructure exists at the scale it does. A nationwide vehicle-movement database was assembled without any national authorization — one police department at a time, through equipment purchases that rarely reached a council vote, let alone a public referendum. Residents of 6,000 communities were enrolled in continuous location tracking by a vendor contract. That also explains why the retreat is happening the same way, municipality by municipality, and why a company response arrives as a product feature rather than a legal concession: self-imposed controls are what a vendor offers when the alternative is losing contracts, and they are revocable in a way that statute is not. The corpus has documented the enforcement leakage the exits are responding to — SFPD illegally sharing ALPR data with ICE (2025-09-08--sfpd-illegal-alpr-data-sharing-ice), Flock pausing an ICE pilot under pressure (2025-08-25--flock-safety-pauses-ice-pilot-program), Texas abortion-related tracking (2025-05-01--flock-safety-texas-abortion-tracking), and a Fourth Amendment challenge to the network (2024-10-21--flock-safety-fourth-amendment-lawsuit). The FBI is meanwhile soliciting $36M for a nationwide warrantless plate network of its own (2026-06-01--fbi-36m-solicitation-nationwide-warrantless-license-plate-network), which is the federal answer to municipal withdrawal: if cities stop buying the surveillance, buy it centrally.
